I struggle to stay motivated to write these long and elaborate posts about exactly what I'm doing all the time or documenting every single event. As has been mentioned before on the blog, In 2019 I wrote a diary every single day for a whole year, and I lost interest and was miserable while doing it for at least 70% of the duration of the "project". So lets not do that again. Let's not burn myself out. I think if I pick something that I do in each month, it should be easy enough to stay on top of writing about it. 

# Why do you care? What am I doing?
Recently I've had to spend a lot of time introducing myself to people. Moving to a new place will often cause this phenomena. So who am I? What do I do? At my first department *director's lunch* (where everyone at Swinburne's Centre for Astrophysics and Supercomputing will gather every Monday for pizza and notices) I introduced myself somewhat as follows: "Hi there everyone, I'm Jade Salisbury. Last year I graduated a Masters' at the University of Manchester, England working on Pulsar Timing. I've just started my PhD here at Swinburne working on FRBs, Magnetars and Pulsars with Marcus \[Lower]"

Initially, this is what Marcus (my supervisor) had introduced me using when showing me around the office on my first day, and I stuck with it because when people asked me what I was working on, I didn't really have a solid idea at the time. Obviously I knew that I was working on FRBs and Magnetars (I applied to the project after all), but I didn't know *how* I was doing that. When other people introduced their researched (and I asked a lot of people what they were doing in my first weeks!) they would mention the methods they are using and the one-line version of their expected outcomes or goals, but I had had none of the details committed to memory yet.

Similarly, I had been out of physics for something like 8 months since I graduated in 2025. So even the stuff that I could read about the goals we had set were a little beyond me. Not to worry though, I told myself, for I had full confidence that my brain just needed a little time to restart, and I was right!

So what am I working on? After about a full month now spent in the office, there's bean a whole heap of events and meetings for me to attend (some related and some unrelated to what I'm doing!) to let my brain get cooking. Now, I can say that I'm currently working on comparing single pulse data taken by Parkes/Murriyang of magnetar J1818-1607, eventually looking for analogues to FRBs. There's heaps of reasons this is interesting and a good area to look into (particularly currently). Additionally, there's plenty of directions for this first project to go. Marcus has informed me that we have data available for 4 of the 6 radio-loud magnetars, and that there's been very little study into all of them as a unit. Similarly, the Parkes telescope data for J1818-1607 has not been studied by anyone either. So there's plenty of new science to do.

# Magnetars and J1818-1607
I finally have a lil' guy! Swift J1818-1607 is a galactic magnetar which also emits radio waves! I spent the entirety of my <a href="{% post_url undergrad %}">Masters project</a> working on generated/simulated data, with no opportunities to look at any part of a real pulsar, so this is a real treat being so hands-on with the data that I'm working on. Similarly, *pulsar timing*, which is the study of precisely calculating the rotational behavior and evolution of a pulsar, mostly utilises arrival time data, or ToAs, rather than the raw telescope data. Now though, I get to work on nearly the direct information provided by the telescope; as a result, the data volumes are *insane*. In the few weeks that I've been here I've already clogged up over a Terabyte of the Swinburne supercomputer, [Ngarrgu Tindebeek](https://supercomputing.swin.edu.au/ngarrgu-tindebeek) (worry not, it's got plenty).

For all of you folks back home wondering how Magnetars are different from pulsars, especially if you remember me as "the pulsar girl", they're still super crazy mega extreme objects spinning (albeit often much slower) like lighthouses in space. When you crank up the magnetic field on a pulsar it appears that a lot of its behaviors can become unpredictable; every process becomes significantly *more* extreme and the result is a wacky object with varying explanations for multitudes of observational behaviour. It doesn't help that compared to standard pulsars, their more extreme magnetar sisters are few and far between.
